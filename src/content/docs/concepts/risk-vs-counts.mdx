## What it is

**Crash count** is how many crashes happened somewhere. **Risk** is how likely a crash is *for each car that passes*, so it needs **exposure**, the amount of traffic.

## Why it matters

A busy motorway junction may have 50 crashes a year and be relatively safe per vehicle; a quiet rural curve may have 2 and be very dangerous per vehicle. Ranking by counts mostly finds busy roads.

Two more traps:
- **Sparse data.** Most places have 0–2 crashes in 12 years, so one unlucky crash can make a quiet road look deadly. **Empirical Bayes** pulls each place's estimate towards the average for similar roads, more strongly when it has little data.
- **Conditions need their own exposure.** Most winter driving in northern Finland happens in the dark, so many crashes in the dark are partly *expected*. Risk in the dark = (share of crashes in the dark) ÷ (share of traffic in the dark).

## How we used it

- **Today's engine** weights crashes by severity, recency (a 5-year half-life) and distance. It's useful for exploring, but it counts crashes, not risk.
- **Design rule 5** in CLAUDE.md: *risk means crashes relative to exposure, never raw counts alone.*
- **Phases 1–2** add traffic volumes, map-match crashes to road segments, and compute Empirical Bayes risk per segment and per condition, with confidence intervals.
- **Recording rules changed** over the years (28,327 crashes in 2014, 8,610 in 2025). Roads didn't get 3× safer, so raw trends must be corrected.

## Check yourself

<details class="check">
<summary>Why can a road with 2 crashes be more dangerous than one with 50?</summary>

Risk is per exposure. If the first carries 100 cars a day and the second 50,000, the first is far more dangerous per car.

</details>

<details class="check">
<summary>What problem does Empirical Bayes solve?</summary>

Small numbers. It stops places with very little data from looking extreme by chance, by blending each estimate with the average for similar places.

</details>
