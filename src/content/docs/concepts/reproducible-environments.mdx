## What it is

A program depends on dozens of packages, each with many versions. A **reproducible environment** means every install (yours, CI's, the server's) gets the **exact same versions**. A **lock file** (`uv.lock`, `package-lock.json`) records them.

## Why it matters

Without a lock, "install the dependencies" can mean different code on different days. A bug appears on the server but not on your laptop, and nobody can say why.

## How we used it

- **uv** manages Python: `pyproject.toml` says what's needed, `uv.lock` pins exactly what's installed, and `uv sync` installs exactly that. Render's `requirements.txt` is *generated* from the lock, and CI fails if it's out of date.
- **Exact pins** in JavaScript: `"react": "19.3.1"`, not `"^19.0.0"`.
- **The two-week rule**: every version was published at least two weeks before it was chosen. Brand-new releases are where breaking bugs and supply-chain attacks most often appear.
- **Data is pinned too**: the database's lock file names its exact SHA-256 ([Data versioning](../data-versioning/)).

<Aside type="tip">
An analogy: a recipe that names the exact brand and batch of every ingredient, not just "flour".
</Aside>

## The trade-off

Pinned versions don't update themselves, so upgrades are deliberate work. That's intended: an upgrade becomes a reviewed change with CI behind it, not a surprise.

## Check yourself

<details class="check">
<summary>What's the difference between pyproject.toml and uv.lock?</summary>

pyproject.toml states what the project needs (often as ranges); uv.lock records the exact versions that were resolved and installed. Installs read the lock.

</details>

<details class="check">
<summary>Why wait two weeks before adopting a new package version?</summary>

Most broken or malicious releases are found and pulled within days. Waiting lets others find those problems first.

</details>
